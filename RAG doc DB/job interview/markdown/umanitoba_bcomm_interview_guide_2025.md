TYPES OF INTERVIEW QUESTIONS
1. Behavioral Interview questions focus on how
the candidate has handled past situations. The
interviewer asks questions based on the
competencies required to do the job. These
questions assess your experience and are
based on the premise that past behavior is the
best predictor of future behavior.
Example: "Tell me about a time when you had
to lead a team under a tight deadline."
2. Situational Interview questions present
hypothetical situations that can occur on the job
and asks the applicant how they would handle
them. This type of interview question assesses
problem-solving and analytical skills.
Example: "What would you do if you were faced
with an unhappy customer?"
3. Techni
cal Interview questions assess specific
technical skills and knowledge related to the job.
Common in fields such as finance, actuary,
accounting, data analytics.
Example: "Can you explain the concept of
discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis and walk
me through how you would use it to value a
company.”
4. Case interview questions are a critical part of
the management consulting recruitment process.
The primary goal is to assess the candidate's
problem-solving skills, analytical abilities, and how
they would approach real-world business
challenges that a consultant might face.
Example: "A client’s sales have dropped by 20%
over the past year. How would you investigate
and address this issue?"

Handling the "What is Your Salary
Expectation?" Question
One of the most challenging questions in a job
interview is "What is your salary expectation?"
It's important to approach this question with
careful consideration, research, and tact. Here
are some steps and tips to help you navigate
this question confidently and professionally.
1. Do Your Research
Before going to the interview, spend time
researching salary ranges for the position you
are applying for. Consider the following
resources:
• Online Salary Databases: Websites like
Glassdoor, PayScale, and LinkedIn Salary
can provide a range of salaries for specific
job titles and geographic locations.
• The Asper Employment Report available on
the website.
• Industry Reports: Professional associations
and industry reports often publish salary
surveys.
• Network: Talk to people in the industry,
including mentors, career services, and
alumni, to get a sense of typical salaries.

• AI-Powered Salary Tools: Utilize AI tools
like Salary.com’s AI-driven salary predictor
or LinkedIn Salary Insights, which can
provide personalized salary estimates based
on your profile and job market trends.
2. Under
stand Your Value
Reflect on your skills, experience, and education
to understand where you fit within the salary

• Your Education: Advanced degrees or

• Relevant Experience: Internships, co-op

• Skills and Competencies: Specialized skills

3. Consider the Whole Compensation Package
Salary is just one part of the compensation
package. Benefits, bonuses, stock options, and
professional development opportunities also
matter. When negotiating a salary, be prepared
to consider other important benefits that add

• Health Benefits
• Retirement Plans
• Paid Time Off
• Professional Development Opportunities
• Flexible Working Conditions
Prepare Your Answer
You generally want to defer answering the
salary question until you receive a job offer.
The longer you can postpone providing a range,
the more leverage you will have when it comes
time to negotiate your salary – ideally when the
employer has determined you are the best
candidate and has offered you the position.
When asked about salary expectations, you
have several options for framing your response:
• Deflect Initially: "I'm more interested in
finding a role that fits my skills and career
aspirations. I'm confident that if we
determine I'm the right fit, we can agree on

• Turn the Question Around: "I'm flexible and
open to negotiation. Can you share the
budgeted salary range for this position?"
• Provide a Range: "Based on my research
and considering my experience and skills, I
believe a salary range of $X to $Y would be
appropriate. However, I'm open to
discussing this further."

ident and Professional. Your delivery is

• Be Honest: Don’t inflate your expectations

• Be Flexible: Show that you’re open to
negotiation.
• Be Positive: Frame your response in a way
that shows you’re motivated and interested
in the position and company.
Practicing your response can help you feel
more confident and reduce anxiety. Consider
answering the salary question by role playing
with friends, family, or career services staff to
refine your answer.

Behaviour Descriptive Interview
Questions & How to Answer
Behavioural-based or behaviour descriptive
interview questions are designed to assess your
past behavior in specific situations to predict
your future performance in similar scenarios,
using required competencies. These questions
typically start with phrases like "Tell me about a
time when..." or "Give an example of..." and
focus on power/soft skills such as teamwork,
problem-solving, leadership, and adaptability.
Why Employers Use Behavioral-Based
Interview Questions
• To Predict Future Performance: Past
behavior is considered a reliable indicator of
future actions.
• To Evaluate Experience: Employers can
understand better how you have handled
real-life situations.
• To Assess Soft Skills: BDI questions help
interviewers gauge soft skills that are crucial
for job performance but not easily
measurable through technical questions.
How to Answer Behavioral-Based
Interview Questions
To answer a BDI question successfully, you need
to tell a specific story, something that happened
in the past. Using the S.O.A.R. formula will help
you structure your answer effectively and tell a
compelling story. The S.O.A.R. template:
S = Situation
Briefly describe the context within which
you performed a task or faced a challenge.
Be specific about when and where this
occurred.
O = Opportunity/Obstacle
Explain what you had to overcome or how
you made things better by articulating the
idea, issue or defining the problem.
A = Action
Detail the specific actions you took to
address the task or challenge. Focus on
what you did personally, even if it was a
team effort. What did you do in this
situation? How did you solve the problem?
What techniques or skills did you utilize?
Include strong action words here like
managed, initiated, developed, and
analyzed.
R = Results
Share the outcomes or results of your
actions. Highlight what you achieved and, if
possible, quantify the results to show the
impact of your actions (#, $, %). Many
interviewees forget this part of their answer
and it’s the most important part!
Example of Using the SOAR method:
Interviewer: "Can you give me an example of a
time when you had to work as part of a team to
achieve a goal?"
Response: "Absolutely. Last semester, I was
part of a team in my Organizational Behavior
class where we had to complete a group
project. The goal was to analyze a company's
organizational structure and present
recommendations for improvement.
Situation: Our team of five was tasked with
analyzing a mid-sized company's organizational
structure and recommending improvements.
The project had a tight deadline, and we had to
present our findings to the class.
Opportunity: As a team, we needed to gather
data, analyze the company's structure, identify
areas for improvement, and create a
comprehensive presentation. My specific role
was to lead the data analysis portion and
ensure our recommendations were well-
supported by evidence.

Illegal Interview Questions and How to
Respond to Them
What Are Illegal Interview Questions?
Illegal interview questions are those that
employers are not allowed to ask during an
interview because they pertain to personal
information that is not relevant to the job and
can lead to discrimination. In Canada, these
questions violate human rights laws that
protect individuals from discrimination based
on factors such as age, gender, marital status,
religion, national origin, disability, and more.
Examples of Illegal Interview Questions
• Age: "How old are you?"
• Marital or Family Status: "Are you
married?" or "Do you have children?"
• Religion: "What is your religion?" or "Do
you observe any religious holidays?"
• Nationality or Ethnicity: "Where are you
from originally?" or "What is your
ethnicity?"
• Disability: "Do you have any disabilities?" or
"Have you ever filed a workers'
compensation claim?"
• Gender: "Do you plan to have children?" or
"How do you feel about working in a male-
dominated field?"
How to Respond to Illegal Interview
Questions Professionally
When faced with an illegal interview question,
it's important to handle the situation
professionally and tactfully. Here are a few
strategies you can use.
Redirect the Question: If asked, "Do you have
children?"
Response: "I’m fully committed to my
professional responsibilities and managing my
time effectively. I'm confident in my ability to
meet the demands of this position."
Seek Clarification: If asked, "Where are you
from originally?"
Response: "Could you clarify how my
background is relevant to the responsibilities of
this role? I’m excited to share how my skills and
experience can contribute to your team."
Address the Underlying Concern: If asked, "Do
you plan to have children?"
Response: "I assure you that I am dedicated to
my career and capable of balancing personal
and professional commitments. My focus is on
contributing effectively to your organization."
Politely Decline to Answer: If asked, "What is
your religion?"
Response: "I prefer to keep my personal beliefs
private. However, I can assure you that my
commitment to my work and my ability to
contribute to your team are my top priorities."
Turn It into a Job-Related Response: If asked,
"How old are you?"
Response: "I have over [X] years of experience
in this industry, and I’m excited to bring my
skills and knowledge to your organization."
